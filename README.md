# cashbackmonitor: Compare Cashback Rates Across 40+ Portals — and Pick the One That Actually Pays Out

People typing "cashbackmonitor" into a search bar usually want one of two things. Either they've heard the name and want to know what the site does, or they already use it and want to know whether the portal sitting at the top of the rate table is really the one they should click. Both questions deserve an answer that goes past "it compares cashback rates."

So here's the short version up front: a cashback monitor tells you who pays the most *today* for the store you're about to buy from. It does not tell you who pays reliably, who has the lowest withdrawal threshold, or who will actually confirm your cashback after the merchant's return window closes. Those are different problems, and they're where most of the money leaks out.

## What CashbackMonitor actually is

CashbackMonitor has been running since 2009 and does one job: it watches the reward rates offered by shopping portals and puts them side by side. As of mid-2026 it lists 41–42 monitored reward portals and covers more than 15,000 stores. Those portals are grouped into four buckets:

- **Cashback portals** — Rakuten, TopCashback, BeFrugal, Mr. Rebates, ShopBack, RebatesMe and similar programs that pay cash.
- **Travel programs** — airline and hotel malls where you earn miles or points instead of cash.
- **Credit card points** — the shopping portals attached to card reward programs.
- **Other reward points** — get-paid-to sites where the earning rate is denominated in site points.

It's free to use. There's a bookmarklet you drag to your bookmarks bar so that when you're on a merchant's page, one click opens that store's rate list. Third-party browser extensions do roughly the same thing. If you create an account, you can assign your own cash value to each airline mile or point currency, and the table re-sorts itself by what those points are worth *to you* rather than by face value. The rate history view is the quietly useful part — it shows when a merchant's offer normally spikes, which is how people decide whether to buy now or wait three weeks.

None of this is a secret, and it isn't a scammy workaround either. Portals earn an affiliate commission when you click through them and buy; they share part of that commission back as cashback. The monitor just sits above that layer and reads the public rates.

## What a rate table doesn't tell you

This is the part that decides whether you end up with money or with a support ticket.

A monitor ranks portals by headline percentage. It doesn't rank them by:

**Payout threshold.** Some portals let you withdraw at a few dollars. Others hold your balance until it clears a higher bar. A 12% rate on a portal with a $25 minimum is worth less to a light shopper than 9% on one you can cash out at $10.

**Confirmation speed.** Cashback goes Pending → Confirmed → withdrawable, and the middle step waits on the merchant. Ninety-day confirmation windows are normal on travel bookings. A high rate that confirms in three months is not the same product as a lower rate that clears in two weeks.

**Exclusions buried in the fine print.** Gift card purchases often don't qualify. Coupon stacking can void a rate. In the US, ShopBack's Amazon offer is a good example of a rate that looks nothing like a percentage: $1 back on your first-ever Amazon order through the platform, $0.50 for each additional order, capped at five rewards per month, on orders of $5 or more, gift cards excluded. That works out to a ceiling of about $3 a month. No monitor table communicates that.

**Tracking reliability.** Ad blockers, switching tabs mid-checkout, or navigating straight to the merchant after having clicked through earlier — any of those can kill the affiliate session, and no cashback appears at all. A rate you never get credited is worth zero.

So the honest way to use a monitor is as a *first filter*, not a verdict.

## The tracking rules that decide whether you get paid

Everything above is theoretical if the click-through doesn't register. The list of ways people lose cashback is short and boring, which is why it gets ignored:

- **Always start from the portal.** Opening the merchant's site directly, even a store you've shopped through ShopBack before, starts a session with no affiliate tracking, and no cashback will appear.
- **Finish in the same session.** Switching tabs, changing browsers, or coming back hours later can break the attribution.
- **Turn off ad blockers for the checkout.** They interfere with the tracking pixel more often than people assume.
- **Read the merchant's exclusions.** Anything marked "up to" has conditions. Hotel bookings on certain rates earn nothing.
- **Don't stack a non-ShopBack new-customer coupon** onto your first order if you're chasing a sign-up bonus — that's an explicit disqualifier.

If cashback doesn't appear within the merchant's stated window, the fix is a missing-cashback claim with your order number, submitted before the claim deadline. Keep the confirmation email.

## How to actually work the monitor and the portal together

The workflow that survives contact with real shopping looks like this:

1. Decide what you're buying, then search that merchant in the cashback monitor.
2. Ignore the top row if its payout threshold or confirmation window doesn't suit you. Sort by *usable* value, not headline rate.
3. Check the rate history to see whether the current offer is a peak or a trough. If it's a trough and you don't need the item this week, waiting is a legitimate move.
4. Log into the portal you picked, confirm the rate on the merchant page — the monitor reads public data and can lag an hour or two — then click through and buy.
5. Squeeze the layers. Sale price, portal cashback, credit card rewards and platform vouchers come out of different budgets and don't cancel each other. That stacking, not the headline percentage, is where the real return is.

Where ShopBack fits: it's worth keeping in your rotation because of the interest rates on travel merchants like Klook, Trip.com and Agoda, and because the payout rules are unusually clear — $10 minimum, bank transfer, roughly 10 days, $300 daily cap. It's licensed as a payment institution in Singapore, which is more than most cashback sites can claim. It is not automatically the winner for every merchant, and any article telling you it is hasn't looked at a rate table lately.

The stubbornly unglamorous conclusion: a cashback monitor answers "who pays most today," and that's genuinely useful. The portals answer "when do I actually get the money." You need both answers before you click.
